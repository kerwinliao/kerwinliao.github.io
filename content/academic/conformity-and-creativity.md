Formal education is widely understood as a system for cultivating knowledge and human potential. Yet its structural logic often privileges conformity, standardization, and the efficient reproduction of established knowledge—paradoxically suppressing the very cognitive dispositions that underpin creativity, originality, and flexible thought. This paper revisits the tension between conformity and creativity from a philosophical perspective, examining how institutional designs systematically reward one mode of cognition while marginalizing others. We develop a pluralistic model of learning that distinguishes competence-oriented from creativity-oriented development—associated respectively with structural and Gestalt cognitive orientations—while treating these not as fixed learner types but as complementary tendencies that most learners combine and that a well-designed system should cultivate together. In response to the asymmetry of current systems, we propose a normative theory of cognitive pluralism, which reimagines education not as a mechanism of selection but as an ecosystem of developmental cultivation. Because creativity is not intrinsically benign, we further argue that its cultivation must be tempered by wisdom—directed toward transformative and ethically responsible ends rather than novelty for its own sake. We conclude by outlining implications for curriculum architecture, teacher formation, and institutional reform, and by identifying directions for interdisciplinary research bridging education, philosophy, and cognitive theory.
